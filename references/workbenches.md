# Workbenches: design, workholding and vises

Read this before designing any woodworking bench (shop bench, pop-up or truck bench, Moxon, sawbench add-ons).
It distils Christopher Schwarz, *The Anarchist's Workbench* (Lost Art Press, 2020; free PDF under CC BY-NC 4.0,
blog.lostartpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/AWB_Consumer_June-2020_v5.pdf), read cover to cover, plus the
Lie-Nielsen Toolworks bench and vise documentation (lie-nielsen.com, read 26 Nov 2026). Everything below is a summary
in our own words; short quotes are marked. Imperial sizes are the book's; metric is rounded.

## The short version
1. **Mass, then stiffness, then workholding.** A bench that slides or shakes while you plane is useless whatever its vises.
   About 60 lb is far too light, things start to work around 150 lb, and above about 250 lb you stop noticing the bench (book, App. A).
   A thick top (4-6 in) makes almost any species stiff enough; don't chase engineering tables.
2. **Clamp anywhere.** The front edge of the top, the front faces of the front legs and the front long stretcher all lie in one plane.
   No apron, skirt, drawer box, door or tool tray in the way of a clamp or a holdfast. Schwarz calls this the bench's "invisibility".
3. **Three tools do 90% of the holding:** a face vise (best: leg vise), a planing stop with an iron tooth, and holdfasts in a
   deliberate hole pattern, plus two cheap appliances: doe's feet and bench hooks. A tail vise is optional, not standard.
4. **Joinery that can't come loose.** "If it can come loose, it will come loose." Blind, massive, drawbored mortise and tenon
   everywhere, including leg to top. No lag screws; bolts only where the bench must knock down.
5. **Sized to body and building.** Length 7-9 ft (2.1-2.7 m) with about 36 in (900 mm) of air at the working end; width about 24 in
   (600 mm); height inside the historical 28-36 in (710-915 mm) band, set by the pinky-knuckle rule.

## History in one page (why the form is what it is)
- **Staked benches** (Roman, 79 AD Herculaneum/Pompeii frescoes; oldest surviving bench at Saalburg, c. 187 AD): a slab on
  3-8 splayed legs wedged into it, often knee or chair height. Holding was the body, nails, pegs, notches, a toothed planing stop
  and a holdfast. Still the best form for green woodwork, drilling chair parts and sitting work; poor for standing joinery.
  Unrestrained legs let a wet top twist (his Löffelholz copy: two corners up, two down, one leg shimmed 1 in).
- **1505, Löffelholz codex, Nürnberg:** the first drawn tail vise (a wagon vise with iron dogs) and a twin-screw face vise, still on a staked base.
  Screw vises appear from the 14th century; the crochet is older than the 1565 date it first shows in the record.
- **Timber-framed bench** (Low Countries and northern France, 1490s-1580s): square legs, four square stretchers, thick top
  slightly overhanging, planing stop, holdfast, often a crochet. Gouda altarpiece c. 1565 (six legs, one stretcher), Vredeman
  de Vries 1572 (full four-stretcher frame), Hieronymus Wierix "Life of the Infant" title page c. 1580 (the clearest drawing),
  Félibien 1676 (same bench a century later, more holdfast holes), Moxon 1683 (double-screw vise, bench screw), Roubo
  *l'Art du menuisier* 1774 (plate 11 is the famous one; plate 279 his "German" bench with leg vise, sliding deadman, tail vise and
  square iron dogs). Calling every such bench a "Roubo" is wrong: Roubo drew a form that was already 200 years old.
- **Germanic lands** pushed mechanisms: tusk-tenoned bases, tail vises with dog strips, shoulder vises (Hallens 1764, Krünitz 1781).
- **Paneled (Nicholson) bench**, Britain, Peter Nicholson 1812: thin wide aprons make a stiff box with little timber. Cheap,
  light, fast, hand-tool friendly; the front apron blocks clamps; a single 2-3/8 in layer of pine top is too springy for hand work.
- **Built-in benches:** fixed to the building's frame (18th-c. Duxbury shop, every 20th-c. garage). Cheap and stout, but you
  can't walk round them or pull them out for an odd job.
- **Cabinetmaker's bench** (late 18th-19th c.): knockdown trestle base on sled feet, laminated top in a skirt, metal-screw
  face vise on parallel bars, dovetailed tail-vise box, square dogs, tool tray. Factory-friendly and fine for delicate work
  when well made; complex, poor for working long edges, and cheap copies are miserable.
- Around 1900 cheap iron quick-release vises pushed out leg vises; Schwarz's 2007 *Workbenches* book and Benchcrafted's hardware
  brought them back. Lie-Nielsen, Benchcrafted and Plate 11 benches are the good modern commercial ones; Ulmia the best 20th-c.
  factory bench.

## Lessons from twenty years of his benches (what went wrong)
| Bench | Lesson |
|---|---|
| Door on sawhorses (1997) | No mass: it walked 3 ft every time he planed a board. |
| $175 yellow-pine bench (2000) | Knockdown bolts never once undone in 20 years; 70 in top too short, 8 ft better. A lamination left in clamps too briefly opened a 1/16 in gap. |
| Power-tool bench (2002) | 53-3/4 in top too short; drawer box under the top blocked clamps and left no room under the top for a holdfast; one twin-screw is not a do-everything vise (screws too close for 24 in carcase sides). |
| 24-hour plywood bench (2003) | Plywood tops stay flat but plane through the face veneer; skirting not worth it. |
| Yellow-pine Roubo (2005) | The form "approaches perfection"; you need lots of clamps for the laminated top. |
| Nicholson (2006) | Wide pine boards cupped after glue-up; single-layer top too springy (fixed with a second layer); apron blocks clamps; aluminium thumbscrew planing stops snapped. |
| Holtzapffel (2007) | Dog holes must be close to the front edge for fenced planes. Removable leg vise + 24 in twin screw is a good pair. |
| LVL bench (2009) | LVL top stays flat but eats edges; bolted lap-jointed base crushed and loosened; lag-screwed top got pushed off by the leg vise. |
| Cherry slab (2010) | Rotted/epoxied slab kept moving and a chunk broke off; slabs take a decade to dry; a 1-3/4 in maple vise chop cracked (use about 3-1/4 in); a door over the shelf turns it into a buried bin. |
| IKEA countertop bench (2013) | Laminated countertops work if dry; finger-jointed look; nuts on a bolted base kept loosening. |

| Löffelholz staked (2016) | Wet wood + no stretchers = twisted top. |
| Ulmia, Moravian (Will Myers) | Tool trays shrink the work area and fight chairs standing on the top; tusk tenons need knocking home; a top that sits on pegs leaps off when you move the bench. |

## Material
- Use the heaviest, cheapest wood you can get easily; compare species by **cost per pound** (cost per board foot x 12 / weight per cubic foot).
  Southern yellow pine came out about $0.23/lb against $1.29 for hard maple (2020 Midwest retail). Yellow pine hardens for years as the resin sets and moves little once dry.
- Avoid: white pine as the only wood (too light, 25 lb/ft3), exotics (expensive and brutal on tools), anything punky or rotted (probe with a knife).
- Myths: colour, open pores and "hardwood only" don't matter; check Janka only to avoid very soft species.
- Let construction lumber sit until it stops moving (weeks); rip case-hardened stock in two half-depth passes with wedges ready.
- Laminated top over slab for most builds: more stable at the start, no forklift, no decade of drying. Orient growth rings in
  face-glued legs so each board's bark side meets the next board's heart side (cup meets bow, seams stay closed).
- Mixed species are fine (hardwood top, softwood base). Air- or kiln-dried both fine; what matters is equilibrium with the room.
- Outdoor, garage or no-HVAC benches: softwoods move less than hardwoods in service.

## Overall sizes
- **Length:** 7-9 ft. Leave about 36 in (900 mm) clear at each end, or at least at the end you plane toward. A 5-6 ft bench in a small room is fine if it has good vises; move the hardware to a bigger bench later.
- **Width:** about 24 in (22 in on the book's bench). 18-1/2 in is tippy when traversing unless braced against a wall; 36-48 in wastes floor and you can't reach the back.
  24 in fits a chair's four feet, a 20 in cabinet side, and lets you reach a rack at the back.
- **Height:** stand relaxed and measure floor to where the little finger meets the hand. Historical range 28-36 in; you can work in about a 3 in band around your ideal.
  If unsure, build tall and saw the legs down. Metal planes want the bench a little taller than wooden planes. Lower benches let the torso drive the plane; taller ones make the arms do it.
  Close work (chopping, carving, layout): sit, or raise the work with a Moxon vise or an étau, rather than raising the whole bench.
  Schwarz is 6 ft 3 in and works at 34 in.
- **Overhang:** top overhangs the base by 16 in at each end on an 8 ft bench, leaving room for clamps or any end vise later ("open format").
- **Shelf:** essential, on cleats inside the stretchers, open (no door, no lid). Stretchers about 4 in off the floor so you can hook a foot under the front one.
- **Weight check:** the book's bench is 307 lb of pine, 353 lb with vise and seat. Lie-Nielsen: 320 lb (7 ft), 420 lb (8 ft).

## The book's bench, as built (reference geometry)
- Top 5 x 22 x 96 in (laminated from 18 strips of 2x12 planed to 1-1/4 in; glued in three six-strip bundles, each left clamped overnight, then the bundles glued together).
- Legs 5 x 5 in, four 1-1/4 in layers; the two middle layers run 3 in long to form a 2-1/2 x 5 x 3 in tenon into the top (2 in of top left above it for a lifetime of flattening).
- Stretchers 2-1/2 x 4 in, 4 in off the floor; long 54 in and short 12 in between shoulders; 3 in tenons made by offsetting laminations; bare-faced (shoulder outside only) 1-1/4 in tenons where the two meet in a leg, one rabbeted 1/2 x 1/2 in so they nest.
- Front of top, front legs and front stretcher coplanar; the back is not a clamping face and needn't be flush.
- Height 34 in. Shelf six 9 in boards plus two 2-1/2 in fillers on 1-1/4 in cleats, screwed, gaps allowed.
- Every joint drawbored (below), glued with liquid hide glue, assembled without clamps. Finish: BLO or a 1:1:1 BLO/thinner/spar varnish blend, nothing on the undersides, never wax or a film.

## Joinery rules
- **Tenon sizing (Moxon's rule, 1683):** make the tenon and the mortise walls equally strong, judged by species and position. In thick stock, tenon = half the member's thickness; when that leaves thin walls, use a bare-faced tenon so the outside shoulder stays thick.
- **Tenons long:** as long as practical; offset the stretcher heights if you want them to pass through.
- **Drawbore:** 5/8 in (16 mm) straight-grained oak peg; hole through the tenon offset 1/8 in (3 mm) toward the shoulder (up to 3/16 in if the tenon hole is eased with a tapered reamer); peg about a third of the tenon length from the shoulder and 1 in from any edge; offset two pegs in a leg so they don't meet; taper the peg's first 1.5-2 in like a pencil and **wax it, don't glue it**. Drive top pegs first, then long stretchers, then short ones, so any gap lands on the short stretchers.
- **Leg to top:** blind drawbored M&T, two pegs per tenon about 1 in below the top's underside. The French through-tenon and sliding dovetail looks good but has no working advantage and goes proud every season. Wood movement of the top is no threat: frames distort a little, they don't tear apart.
- **Avoid:** lag screws (strip with one turn too many), wood screws into end grain, half-laps, dowels, butt-joined 2x4 frames, gravity-and-pins tops on a bench that gets moved. Knockdown (bench bolts with captured nuts, tusk tenons) only for a bench that really travels, and it needs re-tightening every winter. A drawbored joint can be knocked apart once, never as tight again.
- **Fit:** tenons should go home by hand ("sink a tenon with your hat"); the pegs do the pulling.

## Workholding for edges and ends (face vises)
- **Which side:** right-hander's face vise on the left front; left-hander's on the right. Plane toward the screw: the work gets tighter instead of being spat out.
- **Notch:** a square notch sawn in the top's front edge or end, plus a shallow softwood wedge (a degree or two). Free and surprisingly good for tenon cheeks and scrollwork; limited for wide boards.
- **Crochet:** a wooden hook on the front edge; great for planing long edges (rest the work on a 2x4 held by holdfasts between the legs); weak for ends. Moxon's "bench screw" adds one screw through it; the shoulder vise is its full form (good for drawer sides, but sticks out about 11 in and complicates the base).
- **Leg vise (his first choice):** a thick chop on a screw through the front leg, pivoting against the leg. No parallel bars, so it holds tapered work and clamps anywhere between chop and leg. The bottom needs something to pivot on: a pinned parallel guide (move the pin only for big jumps in thickness), a toe-turned second screw, a wedge, or best a **Crisscross** (X-linkage, Benchcrafted, after a 19th-c. original) which removes the guide and the stooping.
  - Book bench: Benchcrafted Classic screw + Crisscross, 3-1/4 in laminated maple chop (3-1/4 x 8 x 35 in), screw set low for 11 in of capacity above it (modern benches put it about 8 in down, old ones 12-15 in).
  - Softwood doesn't hold machine-screw taps; fix hardware with No. 14 wood screws, or through-bolts with washers, never lags.
  - A well-built leg vise with a grippy liner holds an 8 ft board on edge without a sliding deadman; support doors and longer boards on holdfasts stored in the far leg. Add a deadman only if your work proves you need one.
  - Wooden or steel screw both fine; double-start threads move twice as fast; clean grease off steel screws and use graphite so they don't mark work.
- **Wooden-jaw face vise / twin screw:** a leg vise turned sideways under the top; twin screws about 24 in apart clamp a carcase side for dovetails. Independent screws need less upkeep than chain-linked ones. A twin screw on the bench is often too low for healthy dovetailing; make it a separate **Moxon vise** that lifts the work about 6 in.
- **Quick-release iron vise:** easy to fit, small jaw (about 3 in outside the bars), bars need constant care; better for metalwork. Patternmaker's vise: only for odd shapes.
- **Jaw liners, worst to best:** cork (shreds), cork-rubber gasket sheet (Benchcrafted "Crubber"), coarse suede. Glue with epoxy. Line the top's front edge too.

## Workholding for faces (the benchtop)
- **Planing stop (every bench should have one):** a stick friction-fit in a through-mortise, knocked up and down with a mallet;
  book bench 2-1/2 x 2-1/2 x 12 in, mortise 11 in from the vise end and about 2-1/2 in from the front edge (traditionally about 3 in), clear of the base and not forcing you to reach over the vise.
  Fit it so a firm mallet blow moves it about 1/8 in. A blacksmith's iron tooth (a spike in a stepped hole) beats nails, wooden "palms", Millers Falls-style spring stops and aluminium thumbscrew stops. Knock a twisted board's end into the teeth and it won't move. Use the driest stock and make a spare.
  With a batten held across the top against the stop (one holdfast hole behind the stop for this) it becomes a wide stop for panels; it also anchors sticking boards and cradles.
- **Holdfasts:** strike to set, strike the back to release. They need a thick enough top, a plumb hole and a close fit (book: 1 in holes and a heavy holdfast; Lie-Nielsen: 3/4 in holes for a 1-1/2 to 4 in top). Drill with a guide block, stop when the lead screw breaks through, finish from below.
  - **Hole pattern (book):** three staggered rows. Back row 3 in from the back edge, rows 7 in apart (so 3, 10 and 17 in from the back on a 22 in top, the front row about 5 in from the front edge), holes 15-16 in apart along each row and each row offset 7-8 in from the next, plus one hole behind the planing stop. The spacing is set by the holdfast's reach (about 7-8 in): roughly twice the reach.
  - Three holes in the far front leg on 8 in centres, the top one about 8 in below the top: holdfast storage and long-board supports.
- **Doe's foot:** a 1/2-3/4 x 6 x 16 in batten with a 90° V notch; notch round the far corner of a board butted against the stop, holdfast the batten, and the board can't rotate when traversing or planing diagonally. Two of them hold curved parts.
- **End vises (optional):** first drawn 1505. European tail vise box (also a small face vise, but sags if hammered on or badly mounted), wagon vise (his favourite end vise: A for function, C for installing), quick-release vise on the end with a wooden chop carrying a dog near the front edge (B/B), surface vises like the Veritas Wonder Dog (can't hold work thinner than the jaw).
  Too much pressure bows the work. Dog holes 2-3 in from the front edge, about 3 in apart. Round dogs are easy to bore and rotate; square need stepped mortises cut while building; steel grips better but wrecks a plane, wood is the reverse.
  Schwarz rarely cranks a tail vise: stop + holdfasts are faster once learned.
- **Leave out:** tool trays (if wanted, hang one on a French cleat off the back), split tops, four rows of dog holes, lids or doors over the shelf, a drawer box tight under the top.

## Accessories that finish the bench (one Saturday)
Shelf, finish, a French tool rack glued and nailed to the back edge right behind the face vise (5/8 x 3 x 24 in wall on three stand-off blocks), two one-piece bench hooks hung on a nail in a leg, two doe's feet, a Moxon vise, a swing-away seat about 17-1/2 in off the floor bolted to the front leg (for chopping and drawing), a swing-arm lamp on a peg base that drops into a holdfast hole (5000 K LED). Later: shooting boards, a sticking board.

## Building notes worth keeping
- Glue the top first, build the base on it. For yellow pine, leave each lamination in clamps 4 h minimum, overnight better; glue one face only but enough to wet both; roll it on.
- Heavy vintage iron bar clamps (notched bar, Acme screw, cranked handle) close gaps that modern clamps can't.
- Build the planing-stop mortise into the laminations with a spacer block, knocked out 30 min after clamping.
- Lay out top mortises from the dry-assembled base upside down on the top, knife round the tenons through masking tape, bore with a plywood corner guide at half speed, chop sitting beside the chisel so you see it plumb.
- Flatten a laminated top last: traverse with a rank jack plane (chamfer the far edge first), check lengthways with a straightedge, finish with a jointer along the grain. The front 8-12 in matters most; hollows are harmless, humps are not.

## Lie-Nielsen workbench (commercial reference)
- Hard maple (or European beech), 4 in solid top, 24 in wide, 7 ft (320 lb) or 8 ft (420 lb), height made to order 32-38 in. No skirt, no tool tray.
- Trestle base: legs 3 in (7 ft) or 4 in (8 ft) square, front stretcher 4-1/8 in wide, both flush with the front of the top. Knockdown: stretchers bolted into the trestles, the top located by two pins and bolted through the trestle top rails. (Schwarz's caveat applies: bolts need re-tightening.)
- **Shoulder vise = chain-drive twin screw:** 1-1/8 in Acme screws linked by sprockets and chain, 12 in between screws on the 7 ft bench (18-3/16 in on the 8 ft, 29-13/16 in maple jaw), 8-8-1/8 in travel, does not rack when clamping outside the screws; designed for a 4 in top (thinner needs blocking and an apron).
- **Tail vise:** 1 in Acme screw, 6-7 in travel, cold-rolled steel three-piece slide and no L-block; jaw block 15-3/4 x 4 x 5-1/2 in set 5-1/2 in in from the side.
- Square dog holes 1.150 x 0.950 in along the front edge; maple dogs with side springs.
- Holdfast holes 3/4 in (ductile-iron holdfast after Peter Ross: 13 in shaft, 6-1/4 in reach, fits 1-1/2 to 4 in tops). Published layout: holes along the back to hold battens as planing stops (offset from a front dog hole), a middle hole for a shooting board or bench hook, front holes spaced for their dovetail vise; three holes on the tail-vise leg from 8-1/4 in below the top on 6-1/2 in centres.
- Dovetail (Moxon-type) vise: maple, 2-3/4 in jaws, 18 or 24 in between screws, 6 in opening, 4 in tall, clamps to the bench edge, leather-lined outer jaw.
- Care: keep the room near 40% RH, oil 1-2 times a year (BLO / spar varnish / turpentine), flatten with a No. 7 or 8 diagonally then along the grain.
- Compared with the book's bench: thinner, more refined vises (twin screw + tail vise vs leg vise + stop), smaller holdfast holes, a knockdown base, and no planing stop. Both agree on coplanar front, no apron, no tray, thick top.

## Checks for any bench we design
- [ ] Mass estimate ≥ 150 lb (70 kg) free-standing, or the bench is fixed to something heavier; check it won't slide under a planing stroke (sliding needs horizontal force > friction; bolt or block the feet).
- [ ] Front of top, front legs and front stretcher coplanar; nothing under the top within a holdfast's shaft length (13 in / 330 mm below the top surface) at any holdfast hole; a clamp fits at every point on the front edge.
- [ ] Face vise on the correct end for the user's hand; screw low enough for the widest board the user works; chop ≥ 3 in thick; liner on chop and top edge.
- [ ] Planing stop near the vise end, clear of base joinery; one hole behind it; holdfast rows set from the actual holdfast's reach.
- [ ] Top thick enough for the holdfast (and the vise hardware's design thickness, e.g. 4 in for Lie-Nielsen).
- [ ] All base joints drawbored M&T (cadcheck: no screws into end grain, no lags); tenons leave ≥ 2 in of top above them for flattening.
- [ ] Height from the user's pinky-knuckle measurement, standing surface included (a raised floor, platform or truck bed counts as the floor).
- [ ] Shelf on cleats, open. No tray, no apron, no lid over storage that work will be stacked on.
